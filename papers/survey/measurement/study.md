# Measuring an all-arrival capture gate under overload

This is a research study, not a Deleuze design or performance claim. The operator's objective remains retention of everything explicitly instrumented to the capture gate, with reliability paid for by the deployment. A benchmark that becomes quieter when the recorder slows, or reports percentiles only for retained successes, can make that objective disappear from its results. The literature below gives ways to expose that mistake. No benchmark was built or run, and no acquired reference was adopted as a dependency.

Acquisition and reading are separate: [bibliography.tsv](bibliography.tsv), [reading-ledger.md](reading-ledger.md), and [references.md](references.md) record the scope. The source observations below are static findings at the pinned revisions; their runtime consequences have not been reproduced.

## Arrivals, service and the measurement population

Schroeder, Wierman and Harchol-Balter distinguish independent new arrivals from a fixed population that submits again after a response and think time. Their experiments show that even equal load and service demand do not make these models interchangeable. Increasing closed-system concurrency is not a general cure; scheduling and service-time variability affect the models differently. This supports independent offered-arrival schedules for a capture-capacity experiment. It does not establish that every production workload is open: explicitly model application feedback too, while preserving what the generator intended to offer. [Open Versus Closed, §§1–2, 5.1](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/nsdi06/tech/full_papers/schroeder/schroeder.pdf)

For later Deleuze experiments, distinguish these quantities in the experiment's evidence, without choosing a crate API:

| Observation | Meaning and possible blind spot |
|---|---|
| Intended arrival | An identity, payload and due time in the offered schedule, independent of completion. A generator may fail to materialize it. |
| Actual gate arrival | The call or message actually reaches the instrumented gate. Comparing this with intended arrivals exposes generator delay and upstream blocking. |
| Payload ownership / publication | The recorder can use the complete payload after the producer returns. This is different from a reserved slot or pointer into mutable caller storage. |
| Write completion | Bytes were handed through a particular I/O path; identify exactly which completion was observed. |
| Promised retention boundary | The stated memory, process, local crash, power-loss or replicated failure boundary has been reached. These are different experiments. |
| Recovered and checked | After the chosen interruption, identities, payload fidelity, duplicates, order guarantees and integrity are checked against the offered ledger. |

These milestones are an analytical vocabulary, not a proposed Deleuze protocol. Some may coincide in a future implementation; some need additional observations. The [fast-capture study](../fast-capture/study.md) supplies the I/O and recovery distinctions that a timer alone cannot establish.

For a fixed cohort of gate arrivals, assign a distinct arrival identity to every attempt that reaches the gate, including retries for the same logical operation. An experiment can reconcile `arrived = retained + terminally failed + pending + unreconciled`, provided the categories are disjoint at a stated cutoff and retained uses one stated boundary. A failed arrival followed by a successful retry produces two gate arrivals to account for. A separate logical-operation view may group them, but cannot remove either arrival from the capture denominator. Unreconciled includes unknown outcomes; a timeout alone does not prove absence from storage. Expose pending ages as well as attempt identities. Successful-event latency percentiles can accompany this accounting but cannot replace it. This reconciliation is our application of the literature, not a theorem from any one paper.

## Coordinated omission: correction does not recreate events

Gil Tene's wrk2 README makes the important distinction between actual dispatch and intended dispatch time. Its pinned implementation computes corrected response latency from a per-connection planned schedule (`src/wrk.c:518`), while also recording actual-start latency. However, response-completion callbacks supply histogram samples; finite connection state, socket errors, in-flight work and stopping must be accounted for separately. The callback increments the completion counter and checks `stop_at` before histogram recording (`:490–505`); do not assume those populations are identical. The README's approximate millisecond scheduling granularity also prevents treating this HTTP harness as evidence for nanosecond capture precision. [wrk2 primary documentation](https://github.com/giltene/wrk2/blob/44a94c17d8e6a0bac8559b53da76848e430cb7a7/README.md)

HdrHistogram stores counts with configurable value quantization; a histogram is not an event archive. Its Java correction path records the observed value and synthesizes additional decreasing values at the expected sample interval (`AbstractHistogram.java:592`). This helps characterize a particular omitted-sample model. It neither recovers lost payloads nor establishes which real gate arrivals occurred. Keep corrected and uncorrected datasets identified, and do not apply correction twice. The ordinary `Histogram` class is unsynchronized; recorders and concurrent variants have different contracts. [HdrHistogram primary documentation](https://github.com/HdrHistogram/HdrHistogram/blob/de84b0a7de2378abfc405da503bf4898e84ea98e/README.md)

The retained London 2013 Tene presentation page is a company upload, but the visible text is incomplete; its complete slide deck was not recovered. The substantive correction discussion here rests on the retained author documentation and inspected source, not on a claim to have watched the whole talk. [Author/company presentation](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/how-not-to-measure-latency-london-oct-2013/27088981)

## A harness is also a system under load

TailBench separates traffic shaping, request queueing, service time and statistics. Its networked harness uses exponentially distributed interarrivals and does not wait for previous responses before offering requests. The paper also calls out sufficient client capacity, network configuration, repeated runs and percentile uncertainty. Its simplified configurations intentionally change the boundary being measured. Those choices are useful evidence to inspect, not permission to equate loopback, integrated and polyglot paths. Its steady-state focus should be complemented by explicit startup, bursts and recovery studies for Deleuze. [TailBench §IV](https://people.csail.mit.edu/sanchez/papers/2016.tailbench.iiswc.pdf)

TailBench++ extends the original's fixed-client lifecycle toward clients joining and leaving and variable rates. We retained the 2025 author preprint and the publisher's 2026 HTML revision; these are different versions. In pinned `harness/client.cpp:214`, requests receive scheduled arrival times. For a generator-late request, `finiReq` switches from `curNs - genNs` to `curNs - startNs` (`:305`), omitting that lateness from the reported interval. The warning block for excessive generator lateness is commented out (`:248`). Independent network sender/receiver threads therefore do not by themselves guarantee that measured tails include generator backlog. The rate-change code also resets the arrival-distribution origin; inspect change semantics before treating it as replay of one fixed schedule. These are source observations, not measured bug impact. [TailBench++ published article and artifact link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-026-00908-3)

## Backlog, batching, drain and recovery

Little's original proof has stationarity, finite-mean and ergodicity conditions. `L = λW` is a useful consistency relationship at a matching population and boundary; it is not a license to infer stable latency from average completed throughput while the queue is growing. A finite run that ends before the slow cohort drains can censor exactly the important observations. The paper also discusses how arrivals and time in the system must be defined consistently, including rejected units. [Little 1961 publisher record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.9.3.383)

Le Boudec and Thiran define backlog from cumulative input minus output and virtual delay from their horizontal separation for a lossless system. Arrival curves bound bursts over intervals; service curves bound what service is actually guaranteed. Their backlog and delay bounds require those assumptions, not merely a measured average rate. For a capture experiment, cumulative retained output must use the same population and units as cumulative input. Compression changes byte counts; event identity accounting remains necessary. A measured service curve on one run is not a universal guarantee under a different device failure or scheduler. [Network Calculus, definitions 1.1.1 and 1.3.1; theorems 1.4.1–2](https://leboudec.github.io/netcal/latex/netCalBook.pdf)

This motivates retaining time series of offered events and bytes, arrived events and bytes, retained events, pending work, and resource use through both overload and drain. Specify whether arrivals stop during drain, how long observation continues, and what happens to uncompleted work at termination. Batch fill thresholds and timeouts can improve aggregate throughput while extending the age of the oldest record. Distinguish batch amortized cost from individual-event latency; include final flush and all relevant waits in the claimed completion boundary. These are study implications, not a selection of batching policy.

Bronson et al. show why a temporary trigger can leave a system in a self-sustaining bad state: retries, cache behavior and expensive error handling amplify work or reduce useful progress. Logging itself appears in the slow-error-handling example. A quiet healthy-state benchmark cannot rule this out. This is a reason to inspect recovery after an imposed pause ends, including how error paths contend with data capture; it does not authorize discarding required capture arrivals as load shedding. [Metastable Failures, §§1–2.3](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf)

Huang et al. distinguish original client load from amplified system load and separate workload amplification from capacity degradation. Their GC example connects queue growth to still lower capacity, and recovery can require serving catch-up work alongside new work. Keep original events, retries, repair traffic and diagnostic traffic distinguishable in later experiments. A report of high processing throughput could coexist with poor retention of gate arrivals. Logical-event goodput is a separate view; retries arriving at the gate remain required capture arrivals. [Metastable Failures in the Wild, §§3.1–3.3](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi22-huang-lexiang.pdf)

Recent work prevents treating the earlier model as closed theory: the SRDS 2024 MSF-Model studies a queuing model for replicated storage, while a June 2026 preprint proposes a causal characterization including oscillatory failures rather than only overload symptoms. Both are retained, but their models/proofs are not fully reviewed here. [MSF-Model v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16181v2), [Characterizing Metastable Faults and Failures v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00942v2)

## Tails, uncertainty and the observer

Dean and Barroso explain how fanout and shared resources turn uncommon delays into common end-to-end waits. Some techniques they discuss deliberately accept incomplete information-retrieval results or defer mutations; those application choices do not transfer automatically to an all-arrival capture obligation. For Deleuze, investigate common pauses, slow shards and background maintenance while retaining the required event population. This study imports the concern about variability, not an application-specific relaxation of completeness. [The Tail at Scale](https://barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf)

Kalibera and Jones distinguish iteration, execution and build variation, initialized behavior from statistically independent behavior, and effect-size confidence intervals from significance tests. Repeating an iteration in one process does not cover variation introduced at process startup or build layout. Averaging blocks changes the measured unit and its variability. Their work concerns throughput-oriented experiments; its particular analysis is not automatically a tail-quantile estimator for correlated overload traces. Preserve temporal information and choose an analysis that matches the actual experiment. [Rigorous Benchmarking, §§3–4, 6](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/33611/45/p63-kaliber.pdf)

Hoefler and Belli emphasize absolute units, appropriate summaries, complete hardware/software setup, timing perturbation and synchronization. Measuring a block and dividing by its size yields a block mean, not the individual-event percentile distribution. Timer overhead and precision can limit the shortest credible interval, while measurement perturbations can propagate between concurrent workers. Their HPC examples justify scrutiny, not a numeric correction to Deleuze timings. [Scientific Benchmarking, §§3–4.2](https://htor.ethz.ch/publications/img/hoefler-scientific-benchmarking.pdf)

Mytkowicz et al. demonstrate that environment size and link order can reverse experimental conclusions through layout effects; randomization and causal checks address a different problem from merely taking more repetitions of one biased setup. We read selected introduction, methodology and mitigation sections, not every result. [Author primary publication page](https://sape.inf.usi.ch/publications/asplos09.html)

NUMA placement is a separate experimental factor from thread affinity. Linux memory policy affects future allocations, with scope and cpuset interactions; pinning a worker after pages have been faulted does not establish page placement. Include producer/consumer placement, page first touch, migrations and device locality in the experimental record where relevant. This is Linux-specific evidence; Apple and other targets need their own verification. [Kernel v6.14 memory policy documentation](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/v6.14/admin-guide/mm/numa_memory_policy.html)

Bird's Ftrace work separates compiled-out, inactive and active tracer configurations, and discusses filtering/discard cost as well as retention duration. It is historical ARM/Linux evidence that the observer has a cost even when it retains little output. Its microbenchmark hardware and workload are not Deleuze targets, and no reported overhead is transplanted here. [Measuring Function Duration with Ftrace, §§1–2, 4](https://www.kernel.org/doc/ols/2009/ols2009-pages-47-54.pdf)

A percentile label is also not a reliability proof. As an elementary illustration, under independent identically distributed Bernoulli failures, observing zero failures in `N` trials has probability `(1-p)^N` at true failure probability `p`; excluding `p >= 10^-9` with a one-sided 95% zero-failure bound requires about three billion trials. This is a calculation under stated assumptions, not a deployment reliability estimate. Common-mode failures, correlated pauses, test duration, failure coverage and recovery semantics remain separate. Histogram precision, sample quantity and funded failure tolerance are three different things.
